NOAA Buoy 46026 — San Francisco Bar

Dominant period. The wave period carrying the most energy, which is the time for two successive crests to pass a fixed point. It is always either the swell period or the wind wave period. It is not the time between sets.

Swell height. Measured at the buoy, miles offshore, in open water. NOAA reports it as the average height of the highest one third of the waves over a twenty minute sample, so many waves are smaller and some are larger. The figure combines swell with locally generated wind waves. Tap True swell for NOAA's separated swell figures.

This is not the height of the wave that reaches you. Waves grow as they move into shallow water, and how far the water runs up the beach depends on the slope of that particular beach. Research on the Oregon coast measured run-up elevations ranging from 26 to 61 percent of the offshore wave height along a single beach on a single day, varying with the slope of the sand. On steep shores, run-up can far exceed the offshore figure.

Swell direction. The direction the waves at the dominant period are coming from, not the direction they are heading.

Wind. The direction the wind is coming from, with speed averaged over an eight minute period.

Air and water. Measured at the buoy, offshore. Not the temperature on the sand.

Definitions: NOAA National Data Buoy Center — Measurement Descriptions and Units. Run-up: Ruggiero, Holman and Beach (2004), Wave run-up on a high-energy dissipative beach, Journal of Geophysical Research.

Known Hazards at China Beach
Long-Period Swell & Set Waves

Read the table above. Any swell period above 15 seconds means danger. Waves travel in sets separated by long, quiet intervals — sometimes as long as 20 minutes of calm. On long period days, stay aware! During the quiet periods between sets, energy is building offshore. People who walk to the water's edge during the lull are swept in when a set arrives.

Source: NPS / SF Fire Dept / NOAA — Joint Coastal Safety Advisory (sf-fire.org); NOAA National Weather Service Beach Hazards guidance

Deceptive Shelter

China Beach feels protected compared to Ocean Beach. On long-period swell days, this false sense of security is dangerous. The cove can focus and amplify incoming energy from certain swell directions.

Source: NPS GGNRA — China Beach / Presidio (nps.gov/goga)

Shore Break

Waves break directly onto the beach with significant force — enough to cause injury and pull people into the water instantly. Shore break is especially dangerous for children and for anyone standing at the water's edge.

Source: NPS GGNRA — Beach Safety Practices (nps.gov/goga); SF Fire Dept / NPS Joint Coastal Safety Advisory (sf-fire.org)

Rocky Boundaries

The cove is bounded by rocks on both sides. Wave surge on the rocks is hazardous, especially on larger swell days. Stay on the sand.

Source: NPS GGNRA — China Beach / Presidio (nps.gov/goga)

Cold Water — Year Round

San Francisco Bay and ocean water is cold year round. Hypothermia can set in quickly. Always swim with a buddy and stay close to shore.

Source: California State Parks — Ocean Safety (parks.ca.gov/OceanSafety); NOAA / NPS GGNRA Joint Coastal Safety Advisory

01Read the swell period before you approach the water.

The number in the chart above is your warning. A period of 15+ seconds means sets are arriving with long, quiet intervals between them — sometimes up to 20 minutes of calm. During the quiet periods between sets, energy is building offshore. People who walk to the water's edge during the lull are swept in when a set arrives.

02Watch the ocean.

Stay on dry sand. Do not approach wet sand or the water's edge until you have watched the ocean and learned the rhythm of the sets.

03Wet rocks or sand mark where the ocean has already been.

If the rocks or sand are wet, a wave reached them recently. Stay above the wet line — always.

04A long wait between waves is a warning.

During long period swells, stay vigilant. The ocean may appear completely calm between sets. Do not be fooled. If it has been calm for several minutes, stay back. Do not walk toward the water.

05Keep dogs on leash.

Keep dogs on leash and away from the water's edge.

06Tell someone your plans.

Leave your destination and expected return time with someone before you go.

Long-period swell travels in sets.

Long-period swell doesn't produce a steady stream of waves. It travels in sets — groups of larger waves separated by long, quiet intervals. At 15+ seconds, those intervals can stretch to 20 minutes of calm. During the quiet periods between sets, energy is building offshore. People who walk to the water's edge during the lull are swept in when a set arrives.

1Find a high vantage point above the water line.

Do not approach the shore first. Watch from above.

2Time the quiet intervals.

Count the seconds — or minutes — between large waves. This is your set interval.

3Watch for at least 15 minutes.

You need to see multiple sets before you understand the rhythm. One quiet stretch means nothing.

4The largest wave in a set is not always the first.

Sets build in size. The final wave is frequently the biggest.

5When in doubt, stay back.

At 15+ seconds period, stay completely clear of the shore and any rocks.

6Long-period swells create “sneaker waves,” sometimes called “sleeper waves,”

because they produce sets of waves separated by long periods of relative calm. That calm can create a false sense of security — people approach the shore and are not anticipating the next set when it arrives, leaving no time to retreat. These circumstances are predictable and deaths from being swept in are preventable.
