NOAA Buoy 46012 — Half Moon Bay

Dominant period. The wave period carrying the most energy, which is the time for two successive crests to pass a fixed point. It is always either the swell period or the wind wave period. It is not the time between sets.

Swell height. Measured at the buoy, miles offshore, in open water. NOAA reports it as the average height of the highest one third of the waves over a twenty minute sample, so many waves are smaller and some are larger. The figure combines swell with locally generated wind waves. Tap True swell for NOAA's separated swell figures.

This is not the height of the wave that reaches you. Waves grow as they move into shallow water, and how far the water runs up the beach depends on the slope of that particular beach. Research on the Oregon coast measured run-up elevations ranging from 26 to 61 percent of the offshore wave height along a single beach on a single day, varying with the slope of the sand. On steep shores, run-up can far exceed the offshore figure.

Swell direction. The direction the waves at the dominant period are coming from, not the direction they are heading.

Wind. The direction the wind is coming from at the buoy, offshore, with speed averaged over an eight minute period and the highest gust in that time, in miles per hour. This beach faces W. Onshore means the wind is blowing from the water toward this beach, offshore means it is blowing from the land out to sea, and cross-shore means it is blowing along the beach.

Tides. NOAA's predicted high and low tides for station 9414275, Ocean Beach, outer coast, in feet above mean lower low water. These are predictions, not measurements. Wind and swell can push water higher than predicted.

Air and water. Measured at the buoy, offshore. Not the temperature on the sand.

Definitions: NOAA National Data Buoy Center — Measurement Descriptions and Units. Tides: NOAA CO-OPS tide predictions. Run-up: Ruggiero, Holman and Beach (2004), Wave run-up on a high-energy dissipative beach, Journal of Geophysical Research.

Known Hazards at Linda Mar
Long-Period Swell & Set Waves

Read the conditions card above. Waves travel in sets separated by long, quiet intervals — sometimes as long as 20 minutes of calm. On long period days, stay aware! During the quiet periods between sets, energy is building offshore. People who walk to the water's edge during the lull are swept in when a set arrives.

Source: NPS / SF Fire Dept / NOAA — Joint Coastal Safety Advisory (sf-fire.org); NOAA National Weather Service Beach Hazards guidance

Strong Rip Currents

Rip currents are not always visible. They can sometimes be identified by calmer water and less white water than the surrounding surf — which can make them look like a better place to swim. They are not.

Rip currents are common along the north end of the beach near the creek outlet. They are not visible from shore and move faster than most people can swim.

Source: California State Parks — Ocean Safety (parks.ca.gov); NPS / SF Fire Dept Joint Coastal Safety Advisory (sf-fire.org)

Active Surf Zone

This is an active surf beach. Non-surfers entering the water are at risk from both waves and surfboards. Stay out of the surf zone if you are not an experienced surfer.

Source: California State Parks — Linda Mar / Pacifica State Beach (parks.ca.gov)

Cold Water — Year Round

Pacific water along this coast runs cold year round. Hypothermia can set in quickly.

Source: California State Parks — Ocean Safety (parks.ca.gov/OceanSafety); NOAA / NPS GGNRA Joint Coastal Safety Advisory

Creek Mouth & Lagoon

Rip currents, especially near San Pedro Creek and the ends of the beach. The creek mouth changes shape.

Source: en.wikipedia.org · abc7news.com

Seasonal Lifeguard Patrols, No Tower

State Parks San Mateo Sector seasonal roving lifeguard patrol in trucks (covers all state beaches on the San Mateo coast). No fixed tower here. The City of Pacifica operates the beach day to day and posts 'no lifeguard on duty', meaning no tower.

Source: parks.ca.gov · castateparks

01Read the swell period before you approach the water.

The dominant period in the card above is your warning. Longer periods mean sets arrive with long, quiet intervals between them — sometimes up to 20 minutes of calm. During the quiet periods between sets, energy is building offshore. People who walk to the water's edge during the lull are swept in when a set arrives.

02Watch the ocean.

Stay on dry sand. Do not approach wet sand or the water's edge until you have watched the ocean and learned the rhythm of the sets.

03Wet rocks or sand mark where the ocean has already been.

If the rocks or sand are wet, a wave reached them recently. Stay above the wet line — always.

04A long wait between waves is a warning.

During long period swells, stay vigilant. The ocean may appear completely calm between sets. Do not be fooled. If it has been calm for several minutes, stay back. Do not walk toward the water.

05Check the tide.

A higher tide lets waves run farther up the beach and closer to the base of bluffs and rocks. The tide chart in the card above shows today's highs and lows.

06Keep dogs on leash.

Keep dogs on leash and away from the water's edge.

07Tell someone your plans.

Leave your destination and expected return time with someone before you go.

08Rip currents do not always look dangerous.

They can appear as calmer, clearer water with less white water than the surf on either side — which can make them look like a better place to swim. They are not. If you see a break in the surf line with calmer water, stay out.

Long-period swell travels in sets.

Long-period swell doesn't produce a steady stream of waves. It travels in sets — groups of larger waves separated by long, quiet intervals. Those intervals can stretch to 20 minutes of calm. During the quiet periods between sets, energy is building offshore. People who walk to the water's edge during the lull are swept in when a set arrives.

1Find a high vantage point above the water line.

Do not approach the shore first. Watch from above.

2Time the quiet intervals.

Count the seconds — or minutes — between large waves. This is your set interval.

3Watch for at least 15 minutes.

You need to see multiple sets before you understand the rhythm. One quiet stretch means nothing.

4The largest wave in a set is not always the first.

Sets build in size. The final wave is frequently the biggest.

5When in doubt, stay back.

On long-period days, stay completely clear of the shore and any rocks.

6Long-period swells create “sneaker waves,” sometimes called “sleeper waves,”

because they produce sets of waves separated by long periods of relative calm. That calm can create a false sense of security — people approach the shore and are not anticipating the next set when it arrives, leaving no time to retreat. These circumstances are predictable and deaths from being swept in are preventable.
